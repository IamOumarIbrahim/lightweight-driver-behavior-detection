\begin{abstract}
Object detection can localize visible evidence inside the cabin, but detection average precision does not establish a temporal driver state. We therefore benchmark single-frame localization of four discrete, momentary driver cues under a fixed subject-disjoint protocol. The primary RGB experiment retains all 15,723 sampled DMD frames from 14 drivers, including 12,722 negatives, and evaluates released YOLO11n, YOLO26n, and D-FINE-N systems across three predeclared seeds. Confidence selection uses validation only; protected testing reports COCO AP, micro- and macro-$F_1$, false detections on negative frames, and synchronized batch-1 latency. For the completed YOLO runs, YOLO11n reaches $91.79\pm0.44\%$ $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$, $83.42\pm1.36\%$ macro-$F_1$, and $68.0\pm1.3$ frames/s. YOLO26n instead reaches the higher $\mathrm{mAP}_{50:95}$ of $56.98\pm0.81\%$. These directions hold for every paired seed, while subject-level $F_1$ reveals substantially larger variation than the seed-level standard deviations. D-FINE-N results and a separate exploratory NIR training-negative exposure study are \ph. The benchmark consequently supports a bounded comparison of complete detector systems for visible cue localization, not temporal fatigue or behavior inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Driver monitoring systems operate under changes in driver identity, illumination, viewpoint, and occlusion. Whole-frame recognition can describe an activity, but it may also exploit identity or cabin context without showing the visual evidence used for a decision. Object detection provides a complementary formulation: it localizes cue-specific evidence, makes false positives and misses spatially interpretable, and measures detections on frames containing no target cue. A single-frame detector also avoids the added latency and architecture of a temporal model when real-time throughput is the experimental focus. This formulation does \emph{not} infer fatigue, intent, duration, or any other temporal driver state.

The Driver Monitoring Dataset (DMD) provides multimodal attention and alertness recordings \cite{ortega2020dmd}, while Drive\&Act contains synchronized color, NIR, depth, and pose streams for fine-grained activities \cite{martin2019driveact}. Both contain temporally correlated video and repeated observations of each driver. Evaluation can therefore be optimistic if neighboring frames or identities cross partitions, natural negative frames are removed, the test set determines the confidence threshold, or latency boundaries differ.

Recent work proposes specialized lightweight networks for in-cabin recognition and detection \cite{baheti2020mobilevgg,wang2024mfyolo,li2024yolosgc,jin2025yolo11cr}. Our objective is different: compare complete, publicly released nano detector systems without task-specific architectural modification. The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a primary subject-disjoint RGB benchmark for four explicitly defined visible cues, with all sampled frames preserved and the annotation structure automatically audited;
    \item validation-only operating-point selection followed by a protected test, three paired RGB seeds, class- and subject-level analysis, false detections on negatives, and synchronized latency; and
    \item a checksum-backed reproduction workflow plus a clearly separated, one-seed NIR training-negative exposure study at 10 frames/s.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and Evaluation Gap}
\subsection{Recognition Versus Localization}
MobileVGG and convolution--transformer hybrids demonstrate efficient distracted-driver classification \cite{baheti2020mobilevgg,li2024covit}. PO-GUISE+ uses pose and object guidance for efficient driver-action recognition in Drive\&Act NIR imagery \cite{pizarro2024poguise}. Such whole-frame or temporal recognition tasks are not interchangeable with the present bounding-box task. Here, a correct prediction must identify both a cue class and its spatial evidence in one frame; no temporal state is estimated.

Detection-specific work modifies YOLO backbones, attention, or multiscale aggregation for dangerous-driving or fatigue cues \cite{wang2024mfyolo,li2024yolosgc,jin2025yolo11cr}. Comparative studies also assess CNNs against transformers \cite{koay2021cnnvit} or several YOLO generations \cite{herath2025yolocomparison}. However, differences in driver separation, retained negatives, calibration, and runtime protocol limit cross-paper ranking.

\subsection{Released Detector Systems}
DETR casts detection as bipartite set prediction \cite{carion2020detr}; RT-DETR makes the formulation practical for real-time use \cite{zhao2024rtdetr}; and D-FINE refines box-edge distributions for improved localization \cite{peng2025dfine}. We compare D-FINE-N with YOLO11n and YOLO26n as shipped systems, including their native training and inference recipes. In particular, the pinned YOLO26 implementation uses end-to-end one-to-one predictions without non-maximum suppression (NMS), whereas YOLO11n uses its conventional NMS path \cite{ultralytics2026}. Thus, this is a system-level benchmark rather than an isolation of architecture alone.

\section{Primary RGB Benchmark}
\subsection{Cue Definition and Annotation}
The RGB dataset contains 15,723 face crops sampled at 1 frame/s from 81 DMD recordings of 14 drivers. One primary annotator labeled the data in a single pass; no independent second-person review was established. Every sampled frame was preserved to avoid post-sampling curation, yielding 3,001 positive and 12,722 negative frames. Table~\ref{tab:ontology} freezes the spatial ontology. Box semantics intentionally differ by cue because the visible evidence differs.

Each frame contains zero or one box. Yawning and hand-over-mouth are mutually exclusive; when a hand covers a yawn, hand-over-mouth takes precedence. Boxes are tight around only the visible target. Occluded, truncated, or out-of-frame anatomy is never estimated, although a partially occluded cue remains valid when visually identifiable. No arbitrary minimum pixel cutoff is applied at $640\times640$.

An automated COCO audit found 15,723 unique images and 3,001 unique annotations, with zero duplicate IDs or file names, orphan annotations, invalid categories, nonfinite or nonpositive boxes, out-of-bounds boxes, or multi-annotation frames. The smallest annotated area was 0.81\% of the frame; 139 of 159 yawning boxes occupied below 2\%. This is a structural audit, not evidence of inter-annotator agreement or semantic correctness.

\subsection{Split, Models, and Training}
An exhaustive $8{:}3{:}3$ subject assignment minimizes the worst relative class-distribution deviation, with root-mean-square deviation as a tie-breaker. The frozen train/validation/test partitions contain 9,087/3,423/3,213 frames. The three test subjects contribute 1,080/920/1,213 frames and 178/183/253 positives, respectively.

COCO-pretrained YOLO11n, YOLO26n \cite{ultralytics2026}, and D-FINE-N \cite{peng2025dfine} use $640\times640$ input, physical batch size 8, four-step gradient accumulation (effective batch 32), 220 epochs, automatic mixed precision, and no early stopping. A fixed patch normalizes the incomplete final accumulation window by its actual sample count. Native recipes are retained: Ultralytics \texttt{optimizer=auto} with a linear schedule, and the official D-FINE AdamW/MultiStepLR recipe. Seeds $\{13,37,73\}$ were declared in advance and no run was selected for presentation.

\subsection{Evaluation and Uncertainty}
The best model-native validation checkpoint is retained. Its confidence threshold $\tau^*$ maximizes validation micro-$F_1$ over $[0.01,0.99]$, with higher precision and then higher $\tau$ as tie-breakers. At IoU 0.5, predictions are greedily matched one-to-one within class. Protected testing reports COCO $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$ and $\mathrm{mAP}_{50:95}$ \cite{lin2014coco}, precision, recall, micro- and macro-$F_1$, and
\begin{equation}
\fd=100\frac{\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{neg}}}{N_{\mathrm{neg}}},
\end{equation}
the number of above-threshold false detections per 100 negative frames. This is a static per-frame count, not a modeled nuisance-alert or user-experience rate; no temporal confirmation or debouncing is applied.

RGB results are the sample mean and sample standard deviation (SD) over the three seeds. Because the subject split is fixed, the SD measures optimization variability, not generalization uncertainty across held-out drivers. We therefore additionally report each test subject and use paired per-seed differences to check whether model directions are sign-consistent; no significance test is claimed for $n=3$.

Batch-1 FP16 profiling uses an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 8-GB GPU. CUDA synchronization brackets model-forward and tensor-to-final-detections intervals. Disk I/O, decoding, preprocessing, and metric computation are excluded. We report p50 tensor-to-final latency and sustained FPS. This hardware does not test claims about CPU or embedded deployment.

\subsection{Class, Subject, and Failure Analysis}
Per-class paired differences refine the aggregate result. YOLO11n is higher for yawning at all seeds, while YOLO26n is higher for hand-over-mouth and phone use at all seeds. The sign for drinking changes by seed; the defensible conclusion is no consistent difference for that class. These observations remain descriptive because test support is only 28--56 instances for three classes (Table~\ref{tab:ontology}).

Subject variation is more consequential. Averaged across seeds, YOLO11n micro-$F_1$ is 94.94\%, 62.41\%, and 94.72\% for the three held-out subjects; YOLO26n obtains 96.11\%, 54.11\%, and 92.29\%. Thus, the fixed-split seed SD cannot represent uncertainty over unseen drivers. Reporting only the pooled test set would hide the difficult subject.

Figure~\ref{fig:qual} shows predeclared seed-13 examples from frozen predictions. Correct detections localize the intended interaction; the shared miss occurs in glare and includes a small drinking target. More importantly, one nominal negative appears visually cue-like. It may reflect a difficult ontology boundary or a missed label, so the structural annotation audit alone cannot establish semantic validity. This example is retained because it is informative about the benchmark's principal annotation risk.

\section{Exploratory NIR Training-Negative Exposure Study}
NIR is a separate secondary experiment, not a co-equal spectral track or a paired domain-shift test. Its source pool contains 3,786 one-second front-top active-NIR ($850$ nm) Drive\&Act snippets from 15 drivers. Twenty-three unilluminated task snippets are excluded before splitting, leaving 3,763. Drinking and phone-use tracklets provide the two positive classes; 10 frames per snippet are sampled at exactly 10 frames/s, cropped to the driver region, resized to $640\times640$, and interpolated at 0.1-s intervals. Unlike RGB, NIR therefore has dataset-specific frame exclusion and interpolated annotations.

A fixed $9{:}3{:}3$ subject partition is shared across every model and condition. Validation and test contain identical ordered examples. Training always contains 270 positive snippets; conditions contain either 540 or 1,620 negative snippets (nominal positive:negative ratios 1:2 and 1:6). The smaller negative pool is a subject-stratified seed-13 subset of the larger. With epochs held fixed, 1:6 also has three times the unique negatives and approximately three times the optimization steps. The experiment is therefore a \emph{training-negative exposure study}, not a causal isolation of class ratio under matched training signal.

All six jobs use seed 13. Frame-level metrics follow the RGB protocol. Because the ten frames within a one-second snippet are near-duplicates, snippet-level operating metrics treat each snippet as one unit: a class is recovered if any frame contains a correctly matched prediction, repeated detections count once, and a negative snippet is false positive if any frame contains an above-threshold detection. Table~\ref{tab:nirresults} will report both granularities after the protected evaluations.

\section{Limitations}
The RGB sample contains 14 drivers and only three held-out test drivers, so subject-disjoint evaluation reduces identity leakage but does not establish population-level generalization. Seed SD on this fixed split measures optimization variability only. One annotator completed one pass without independent review; the audit verifies file and geometric constraints but not semantic agreement, and the qualitative analysis exposes at least one ambiguous or potentially missed label. Frames from the same recording remain correlated, and no clip-level uncertainty is claimed for RGB. The single-frame task does not model temporal fatigue or alert logic.

The NIR experiment differs in dataset, ontology, annotation source, sampling rate, and preprocessing, uses one seed, and confounds negative diversity with training exposure. Its results cannot be compared with RGB as a controlled modality effect. Finally, all models retain system-specific optimization and postprocessing, while RTX 4060 latency excludes preprocessing and is not an embedded-device measurement. These constraints limit conclusions to the declared systems, data, and metrics.

\section{Conclusion}
The completed RGB comparison shows a reproducible trade-off: YOLO11n provides the stronger validation-calibrated operating point and throughput, while YOLO26n provides higher strict-IoU AP through its distinct end-to-end system. Paired seeds support those aggregate directions, but class and subject analyses prevent overgeneralization. The final three-model RGB ranking and the separate NIR exposure findings are \ph. The resulting contribution is a transparent benchmark for spatial localization of visible driver cues, not a claim of temporal driver-state inference.
